*This is general information, not legal advice. Wills are governed by state law, and the right steps for you depend on where you live and your circumstances.*

Preparing to write a will is mostly a gathering exercise. Before you draft anything, collect the facts about who you are and what you have. The sections below walk through what a state-agency checklist suggests you assemble, then what makes the finished document validly signed.

## Why should I prepare a will at all?

Because without one, the state decides for you. A will lets you decide what happens to your money and possessions at death and who becomes guardian of your minor children; if you don't make one, state laws and courts make those decisions, according to the [Maine Estate Planning Checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf).

That is the practical reason a checklist helps. Having your information and wishes organized before you start makes drafting go more smoothly.

## What personal information should I gather first?

Start with the basics that identify you and your family. The [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf) tells you to list personal data, including:

- Your Social Security number
- Your driver's license number
- Your date of birth
- Family contacts

Write these down in one place so they are ready when you begin.

## What financial information do I need to list?

List your income sources and your accounts, then check that everything lines up. The [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf) says to list income sources and government benefits such as pensions and Social Security. It also advises reviewing your financial accounts and insurance policies so they conform to your estate planning arrangements.

That last point is easy to skip. A will is only one piece of an estate plan, and your accounts and policies should be reviewed alongside it so the pieces fit together rather than conflict. For a practical walk through the kinds of questions a drafting process asks, see our guide to [what a will questionnaire actually asks](/blog/will-questionnaire-what-it-actually-asks).

## What should I think through before I start drafting?

Know what a will is for. According to the [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf), a will lets you decide what happens to your money and possessions at death and who becomes guardian of your minor children.

With that in mind, it helps to arrive at the drafting stage with your own answers ready to questions like these (these prompts are our suggestions, not items from the checklist):

- Who do you want to receive your property?
- If you have minor children, who would you want as their guardian?

You don't need perfect answers on day one, but a draft goes faster when you have thought them through.

## Should I make a living will too?

It is worth considering as a separate document. The [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf) lists making a living will as its own step; it outlines the medical procedures you want if you are too ill to state your wishes.

A living will and a will do different jobs, so preparing for one does not complete the other. We explain the distinction in detail in [Living Will vs. Last Will and Testament](/blog/living-will-vs-last-will-and-testament).

## What does it take to sign a will properly?

Under the Massachusetts version of Uniform Probate Code § 2-502, a will must be in writing, signed by you, and signed by at least two witnesses. Specifically, it must be signed by the testator, or by another person in the testator's conscious presence and at their direction. It must also be signed by at least two individuals, each of whom witnessed either the testator's signing or the testator's acknowledgment of the signature or the will. See [G.L. c. 190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502).

The same statute adds that intent that the document be the testator's will can be established by extrinsic evidence. That is a safety valve, not a plan. Treat it as a reason to be careful, not a reason to be casual.

Keep one caution in mind: this is one state's adoption of the Uniform Probate Code and may differ from your state's law. For a closer look at the model text, read our post on [what UPC § 2-502 actually requires](/blog/upc-2-502-what-it-actually-requires).

## What should I do once my checklist is complete?

Review your plan with advisers. The [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf) advises consulting legal, financial and tax advisers, and that advice is sensible for anyone with a complicated situation.

Once your information is gathered, you can [start building your will here](/create).

## Quick checklist recap

- Personal data: Social Security number, driver's license number, date of birth, family contacts, per the [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf)
- Income sources and government benefits, such as pensions and Social Security
- Financial accounts and insurance policies, reviewed against your plan
- Your own answers on who receives your property and, if applicable, who would be guardian of minor children
- A living will as a separate document for medical wishes
- Signing in line with your state's execution rules, including witnesses; in Massachusetts, see [G.L. c. 190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502)
- Advice from legal, financial and tax advisers

## FAQ

**What do I need to have ready before I make a will?**
Gather your personal data (Social Security number, driver's license number, date of birth, family contacts), your sources of income and government benefits, and your financial accounts and insurance policies. The [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf) lists these items and advises consulting legal, financial and tax advisers.

**Why does it matter whether I have a will at all?**
A will lets you decide what happens to your money and possessions at death and who becomes guardian of your minor children. Without one, [state laws and courts decide](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf).

**What makes a will validly signed?**
Under the Massachusetts version of UPC § 2-502, a will must be in writing, signed by you (or by someone at your direction in your conscious presence), and signed by at least two witnesses. See [G.L. c. 190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502). Other states may differ.

**Is a living will the same as a will?**
No. The [Maine checklist](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf) treats a living will as a separate step covering the medical procedures you want if you are too ill to state your wishes.

**Do these rules apply in every state?**
Not necessarily. The Massachusetts statute ([G.L. c. 190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502)) is one state's adoption of the Uniform Probate Code, and the [Maine document](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/kennebec_woodlands/downloads/documents/estate_planning_checklist.pdf) is general guidance from a state agency rather than a statute. Check your own state's law.
