## Is a will made with an online will creator legally valid?

It can be, if the finished document meets your state's execution rules. Validity turns on how the will is signed and witnessed under your state's law.

Massachusetts offers one concrete example. [G.L. c. 190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502) requires that a will be in writing, signed by the testator (or by someone else in the testator's conscious presence and at the testator's direction), and signed by at least two individuals who witnessed the signing or the testator's acknowledgment. Other states' rules differ, so check yours.

## What does Massachusetts require for a will to be properly executed?

Massachusetts requires three things: a writing, the testator's signature, and at least two witnesses. The statute at [G.L. c. 190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502) lists exactly these elements.

Two details from that section are easy to miss:

- **Someone else can sign for you.** The signature may be made by another person in your conscious presence and at your direction.
- **Witnesses can see the signing or your acknowledgment.** They must be at least two individuals, and they can witness either the act of signing or your acknowledgment of the signature or the will.

Whether another state uses the same elements is something you have to confirm in that state's own statute.

## Can a court look beyond the document to find my intent?

In Massachusetts, in at least one respect, yes. [Massachusetts § 2-502(b)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502) provides that intent that a document is the testator's will can be established by extrinsic evidence. This article does not describe how other states treat the question, so do not assume your state works the same way.

## Can I skip witnesses and just handwrite or e-sign it?

It depends on the state, and this article does not state any state's rule on handwritten or electronic wills. Rules on those formats vary by state, so check your own state's statute.

Before you rely on an e-signature, a remote signing, or a handwritten document, read your state's current probate statute. The Massachusetts requirement of two witnesses, shown in the [statute text](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502), applies to Massachusetts; confirm what applies where you live.

## What happens if I don't make a will?

You die intestate, and state law decides who gets your property. [Cornell's Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/Intestacy) explains that intestacy means dying without a will, that the estate goes through probate court, and that the state's intestacy rules determine who inherits, typically relatives of the decedent.

The [Alaska Court System's self-help page](https://courts.alaska.gov/shc/probate/intestacy.htm) adds that the probate process is generally the same whether or not there is a will; what changes is that state law decides who receives the property. Alaska gives one example: if the person left a surviving spouse but no descendants, all of the property goes to the surviving spouse. That is Alaska's rule, and intestacy rules vary by state, so your result could be different.

## What should I confirm before relying on any will?

Check your own state's rules before treating any document as a valid will:

1. **Look up your state's execution rules** in its probate statute. Massachusetts, for example, spells out [the writing, signature, and witness elements](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502).
2. **Sign it the way your state requires**, with the number of witnesses your state requires.
3. **Understand the fallback.** If a will is not valid, [intestacy rules](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/Intestacy) determine who inherits, and the result may not match what you wanted.

If you are unsure whether a document meets your state's requirements, a licensed attorney in your state can advise you.

## FAQ

**Is a will made with an online will creator legally valid?**
It can be, if the finished document meets your state's execution rules. Massachusetts, for example, requires a will to be in writing, signed by the testator, and signed by at least two individuals who witnessed the signing or the testator's acknowledgment, per [G.L. c. 190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502). Other states' rules differ, so check your own state's statute.

**Can I skip the witnesses and just e-sign my will?**
That depends on your state, and this article does not state any particular state's electronic-will or handwritten-will rule. Massachusetts, for one, requires [at least two witnesses](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502); confirm your own state's current statute before relying on an e-signature or a handwritten document.

**What happens if I never finish my will?**
If you die without a valid will, you die intestate. The estate goes through probate court, and your state's intestacy rules decide who inherits, typically relatives of the decedent, as [Cornell's Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/Intestacy) explains.

**Does every state have the same will rules?**
No. Execution formalities, witness requirements, and intestacy rules vary by state. The [Massachusetts statute](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502) and the [Alaska intestacy example](https://courts.alaska.gov/shc/probate/intestacy.htm) illustrate how particular states' laws work, not a nationwide rule.
