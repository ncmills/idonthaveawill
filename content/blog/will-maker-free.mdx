*This is general information, not legal advice. Wills are governed by state law, so confirm the rules where you live.*

A "will maker free" search usually means one thing: you want a will without paying for one. That is a reasonable goal, and this page does not tell you whether any particular free tool is good or bad. What it does is lay out what the sources say about dying without a will and about self-proving wills, so you can judge any template, free or paid, with those points in mind.

## Is a will from a free will maker legally valid?

We did not find a primary source that evaluates free will makers as a category, so we won't claim they are valid, safe, or unsafe. The sources reviewed do not address the quality of any particular tool or the documents it produces.

What the sources do cover is what happens without a valid will and how a will can be made self-proving. As background, Cornell's legal dictionary [defines intestacy](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intestacy) as dying without a will, with the state's intestacy rules deciding who inherits. If you use a free tool, check its output against your own state's rules for making a will, and see the sections below for the points that the sources do support.

## What happens if I die without a valid will?

Your estate goes through probate court, and your state's intestacy rules decide who inherits. Cornell's legal dictionary [defines intestacy](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intestacy) as dying without a will, with the state's rules determining who takes the property.

Under those rules, the people who inherit are typically relatives of the person who died, and a person must survive the decedent to inherit, according to [Cornell's Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intestacy).

States describe this in their own terms. Alaska's court system says that when there is no valid will, [Alaska state law determines who inherits](https://courts.alaska.gov/shc/probate/intestacy.htm). Maryland's Register of Wills says a person who dies without a valid will or other testamentary writing is "intestate," and the estate is distributed under [Maryland's intestate succession law](http://registers.maryland.gov/main/intestate.html), which generally follows bloodlines and degrees of consanguinity. We are not stating how any estate would be split, because those shares vary by state.

## Does dying without a will skip probate?

No. Probate still happens. Alaska's court system explains that probate for an intestate estate is [generally the same process as for a will](https://courts.alaska.gov/shc/probate/intestacy.htm), including paying creditor claims and transferring property to heirs. The main difference is that state law decides who gets the property.

So skipping a will does not avoid court involvement.

## What is a self-proving will, and do I need one?

A self-proving will is a regular will that meets extra requirements so it can be validated without witness testimony in probate court. Cornell's Wex says that in most states this is done with [witness affidavits signed before a notary](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/self-proving_will).

The sources we reviewed do not say whether a self-proving affidavit is required. What they do show is the benefit: Florida's Department of State says making a will self-proving [shortens and simplifies the probate steps](https://notaries.dos.fl.gov/education/faq/will.html).

Florida also shows how a state can prescribe the process. According to the [Department of State](https://notaries.dos.fl.gov/education/faq/will.html), §732.503 of the Florida Statutes sets how a will can be self-proved: the testator and witnesses take an oath and sign an affidavit that they signed in each other's presence. If your free will maker offers a self-proving option, compare its wording against your own state's version.

Availability also varies. Cornell's Wex says self-proving wills are available in all states except the District of Columbia, Maryland, Ohio, and Vermont, but that [page was last reviewed in August 2021](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/self-proving_will) and may have changed. Treat that list as a starting point and confirm it locally.

## Can the notary also be my witness?

In Florida, no. Florida's Department of State guidance says that because witness signatures are also notarized on a self-proved will, [the notary may not serve as one of the witnesses](https://notaries.dos.fl.gov/education/faq/will.html). The same guidance says the notary is not responsible for supplying the two witnesses.

This is a Florida rule, and we are not claiming other states handle it the same way. If you plan to have a will notarized, look up your own state's rules before the signing day.

## How should I use a free will maker safely?

Use the sources above as a checklist of questions to answer for your own state, and keep in mind that wills are governed by state law:

- Look up your state's rules for making a will, usually in its probate code or on the probate court's website. The sources reviewed here do not spell out those rules.
- Consider whether to make the will self-proving. If so, look up your state's affidavit and notary process, such as [Florida's](https://notaries.dos.fl.gov/education/faq/will.html), which is described by that state's Department of State.
- Some people choose to consult an attorney licensed in their state, particularly if their situation is complicated.

**Is a will from a free will maker legally valid?**

**What happens if I die without a valid will?**
Your estate goes through probate court, and [intestacy rules](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intestacy) decide who inherits, typically relatives who survive you.

**What is a self-proving will?**
It is a regular will that meets extra requirements so a court can validate it without witness testimony, usually through [notarized witness affidavits](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/self-proving_will).

**Does every state allow self-proving wills?**
