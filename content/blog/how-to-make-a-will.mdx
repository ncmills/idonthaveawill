*This is general information about wills, not legal advice. Probate law differs by state, so confirm the rules that apply to you with your state's statutes or a licensed attorney.*

Making a will is a matter of following your state's rules. The details differ from state to state, so this guide uses statutes from Massachusetts, Utah, and Idaho as examples and points you back to your own state's probate code for the final word.

## What do you need for a valid will?

Under Massachusetts law, a will must be in writing, signed, and witnessed by at least two people. Specifically, under [Massachusetts General Laws ch. 190B, § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502), a will must be:

- **In writing**
- **Signed by the testator** (the person making the will), or signed by another person in the testator's name
- **Signed by at least two individuals** who witnessed either the signing itself or the testator's acknowledgment of the signature or the will

That section follows the Uniform Probate Code (UPC) model, but you should not assume every state matches it. Each state's statute governs, and some states add requirements or allow alternatives. This article does not cover which states accept handwritten wills, whether witnesses must be "disinterested," or whether notarization can substitute for witnesses, because those rules vary and should be checked against your own state's code.

One more point from the Massachusetts statute: [intent that a document be the testator's will can be established by extrinsic evidence](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502). In other words, the law cares about whether the document was meant to be your will, and evidence beyond the page itself can be used to show that.

## How old do you have to be to make a will?

In Utah, you must be 18 or older. According to the Utah Code, [Title 75, Chapter 2, Part 5](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-P5_1800010118000101.pdf), an individual may make a will if they are 18 or older and have testamentary capacity when the will is executed. (Cited at § 75-2-501; this link should be re-verified on le.utah.gov before publication.)

Two things are packed into that sentence:

- **Age:** 18 is the Utah threshold. We have confirmed it only for Utah, so if you live elsewhere, look up your state's rule rather than assuming it is the same.
- **Testamentary capacity:** Utah requires testamentary capacity when the will is executed.

## Can a will be "self-proved"?

Utah's statute describes a way to do it. [Utah Code § 75-2-504](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-P5_1800010118000101.pdf) lets a will be made "self-proved" through the testator's acknowledgment and the witnesses' affidavits, each made before an officer authorized to administer oaths.

We have not confirmed how widely other states accept self-proving affidavits or whether they are optional everywhere, so ask about this when you check your state's rules.

## How do you revoke or replace a will?

Under the Idaho statute, and under the Utah text as quoted in the research for this article, a will can be revoked by a later will or by a physical act done with intent to revoke. Massachusetts addresses the revocatory act, as described below.

- **Idaho:** under [Idaho Statutes § 15-2-507](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title15/t15ch2/sect15-2-507/), a will is revoked by a later will that revokes it expressly or by inconsistency, or by being burned, torn, canceled, obliterated, or destroyed with intent to revoke.
- **Utah:** Utah's version of the same rule, § 75-2-507, provides that a will or part of it is revoked by a later will that revokes it expressly or by inconsistency, or by a "revocatory act" such as burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating, or destroying it, if done with intent to revoke. We were not able to verify this section on le.utah.gov at the time of writing, so confirm it in the current [Utah Code, Title 75, Chapter 2](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-P5_1800010118000101.pdf) before relying on it.
- **Massachusetts:** the Massachusetts version of § 2-507 provides that a revocatory act counts if the testator did it with intent to revoke, or if another person did it in the testator's conscious presence and at the testator's direction. We could not confirm a live link to that section at the time of writing, so check ch. 190B, § 2-507 on the [Massachusetts Legislature's site](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502) (the same chapter as § 2-502) for the current text.

Whatever your state, intent is part of the Utah, Idaho, and Massachusetts rules described above: a physical act counts as revocation only when done with the intent to revoke.

## What happens if you die without a will?

You die "intestate," and state law decides who gets your property. According to [Cornell's Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/Intestacy), the estate goes through probate court, state intestacy rules decide who inherits, and the takers are typically relatives who survive the decedent.

Outcomes depend on your state. We have not confirmed a uniform national order of heirs, so look up your own state's intestacy statute to see who would inherit in your situation.

## What should you do next?

Look up your own state's probate code and read the execution requirements for wills. The Massachusetts and Utah examples above show some of the building blocks (a writing, a signature, witnesses, capacity, and a way to revoke), but your state's statute is the controlling text. For example, you can read the execution rule yourself in [Massachusetts General Laws ch. 190B, § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502) and compare it with the statute in your state.

When you are ready to put your wishes into a document, you can [start building your will here](/create). If you would like help, consider consulting a licensed attorney in your state.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does a will need to be valid?** Under Massachusetts's [§ 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502), a will must be in writing, signed by the testator (or by another person in the testator's name), and signed by at least two witnesses. Other states set their own rules.

**How old do I have to be to make a will?** In Utah, [18 or older](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-P5_1800010118000101.pdf), with testamentary capacity when the will is executed. This is confirmed only for Utah.

**How do I cancel an old will?** Under [Idaho § 15-2-507](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title15/t15ch2/sect15-2-507/), a will is revoked by a later will that revokes it expressly or by inconsistency, or by burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating, or destroying it with intent to revoke. Utah's § 75-2-507 is described to the same effect (verify it in the [Utah Code](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-P5_1800010118000101.pdf)). Massachusetts treats a revocatory act as effective if the testator did it with intent to revoke, or if another person did it in the testator's conscious presence and at the testator's direction; see [ch. 190B](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502).

**What happens if I die without a will?** Your estate goes through probate and [state intestacy rules](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/Intestacy) decide who inherits, typically surviving relatives. Rules differ by state.

**Does a will have to be self-proved?** Utah's [statute](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-P5_1800010118000101.pdf) describes a self-proving mechanism using the testator's acknowledgment and witnesses' affidavits before an officer authorized to administer oaths. Whether it is optional or accepted in your state is something to confirm locally.
