# Uniform Probate Code Section 2-502: What It Actually Requires for a Valid Will

Section 2-502 is the execution provision of the Uniform Probate Code — prepared by the Uniform Law Commission in 1969, last amended in 2019, and enacted in whole or in part in [18 jurisdictions](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_probate_code) per Cornell's Legal Information Institute. No will is probated under "UPC § 2-502"; it is probated under a state's enacted copy. What follows reads five of them side by side.

## What does Uniform Probate Code 2-502 actually require?

**Three things, and the section stops there.** As enacted, § 2-502(a) requires a will to be (1) in writing; (2) signed by the testator, *or* in the testator's name by another individual "in the testator's conscious presence and by the testator's direction"; and (3) signed by **at least 2 individuals**, each of whom witnessed either the signing or the testator's acknowledgment of that signature or of the will. The language is materially identical in [MCL 700.2502(1)](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-700-2502.pdf), [MGL c.190B § 2-502(a)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502), [Utah Code § 75-2-502(1)](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S502_1800010118000101.xml) and [Me. tit. 18-C § 2-502(1)](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/18-C/title18-Csec2-502.html).

One deadline is *not* uniform: Michigan, Utah and Maine each require a witness to sign "within a reasonable time after" witnessing the signing or acknowledgment. The Massachusetts version of that clause omits the phrase entirely.

## Do the witnesses have to watch the signing, or be in the room together?

**No — and this is the most-missed feature of the section.** A witness satisfies § 2-502(a)(3) by having seen *either* the signing *or* the testator's acknowledgment of the signature or of the will. Across the complete enacted text of [MCL 700.2502](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-700-2502.pdf), [MGL c.190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502), [Utah Code § 75-2-502](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S502_1800010118000101.xml) and [Minn. Stat. § 524.2-502](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/524.2-502), three familiar requirements are absent: the witnesses need not sign in the testator's presence, need not be in each other's presence, and the testator need not declare the document to be a will. That is a drafting choice — the non-UPC states below impose exactly those requirements in their own words.

## Is a handwritten will valid under section 2-502?

**Under the holographic subsection, yes — an unwitnessed will counts "whether or not witnessed, if the signature and material portions of the document are in the testator's handwriting."** That is the text at [Utah Code § 75-2-502(2)](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S502_1800010118000101.xml) and [Me. tit. 18-C § 2-502(2)](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/18-C/title18-Csec2-502.html), neither of which requires a date. [Michigan adds one](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-700-2502.pdf): MCL 700.2502(2) validates a holographic will only "if it is dated."

Massachusetts went the other way: [MGL c.190B § 2-502](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502) has no holographic exception at all — though a handwritten will valid where it was executed can still come in through the choice-of-law section below.

A final subsection lets testamentary intent be shown by extrinsic evidence, "including, for a holographic will, portions of the document that are not in the testator's handwriting" ([MCL 700.2502(3)](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-700-2502.pdf)).

## Does section 2-502 forgive a will that was signed wrong?

**No. That is § 2-503, a different section — and it is not everywhere.** Harmless error treats a defectively executed document as valid only if the proponent establishes **by clear and convincing evidence** that the decedent intended it as a will, a revocation, an addition or alteration, or a revival ([Utah Code § 75-2-503](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S503_1800010118000101.xml)). Three cautions:

- Massachusetts adopted the code but left [§ 2-503 **"Reserved"**](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-503) — the number exists with no operative text.
- Minnesota's version reaches only "documents and writings executed on or after **March 13, 2020**" ([Minn. Stat. § 524.2-503(b)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/524.2-503)).
- Section numbers do not travel intact: in Maine's 2017 recodification, [§ 2-503 is the self-proved-will provision](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/18-C/title18-Csec2-503.html), and Maine's § 2-502 cross-references §§ 2-505 and 2-512.

## Who may make a will, and who may witness one?

**The companion provision, § 2-501, sets the threshold at "an individual 18 or more years of age who is of sound mind"** — word for word the same in [MGL c.190B § 2-501](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-501) and [Utah Code § 75-2-501](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S501_1800010118000101.xml).

Witness eligibility is § 2-505, and Massachusetts departs sharply. Under [Utah Code § 75-2-505](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S505_1800010118000101.xml), anyone generally competent to be a witness may witness a will, and "the signing of a will by an interested witness does not invalidate the will or any provision of it." [MGL c.190B § 2-505(b)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-505) instead voids a devise to a witness or the witness's spouse unless there are two other subscribing witnesses, or unless the interested witness proves the devise was not the product of fraud or undue influence.

A signature on a self-proving affidavit, meanwhile, "is considered a signature affixed to the will, if necessary to prove the will's due execution" ([MGL c.190B § 2-504(c)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-504)).

## Why does a state's rule look different from section 2-502?

**Because § 2-502 is a model — and because it is not self-contained: its own opening clause carves out exceptions.** Michigan's opens "Subject to section 1202, and except as provided in" §§ 2503, 2506 and 2513 ([MCL 700.2502(1)](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-700-2502.pdf)); Minnesota's points to §§ 524.2-506 and 524.2-513 ([Minn. Stat. § 524.2-502](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/524.2-502)).

Section 2-506 is why a will signed elsewhere can still work: a written will is valid if it complies with § 2-502 **or** with the law of the place of execution, or of the place where the testator was domiciled, had a place of abode, or was a national — at execution or at death ([MGL c.190B § 2-506](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-506)).

## How do California, New York and Florida differ?

**None of the three is a Uniform Probate Code state, and each adds a formality § 2-502 leaves out.**

- **California:** two witnesses "being present at the same time" who witness the signing or acknowledgment **and** who understand that the instrument is the testator's will ([Cal. Prob. Code § 6110(c)(1)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PROB&sectionNum=6110)) — neither requirement appears in § 2-502. Harmless error sits *inside* § 6110 as subdivision (c)(2), and the holographic test asks whether the "material **provisions**" are handwritten ([§ 6111(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PROB&sectionNum=6111)) — a different word from "material portions of the document." More: [California will requirements](/blog/ca-wills).
- **New York:** signed "at the end thereof"; the testator must declare to each attesting witness that the instrument is their will; both witnesses must attest "within one thirty day period" ([N.Y. EPTL § 3-2.1](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/EPT/3-2.1)). Holographic wills reach only armed-forces members in actual military or naval service during a declared or undeclared war, those serving with or accompanying them, and mariners at sea ([§ 3-2.2](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/EPT/3-2.2)) — see [traditional and holographic wills in New York](/blog/comparing-traditional-and-holographic-wills-ny).
- **Florida:** witnesses must "sign the will in the presence of the testator and in the presence of each other," and a handwritten will executed under subsection (1) "shall not be considered a holographic will" ([Fla. Stat. § 732.502(1)(c), (2)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/732.502)).

Which rules govern is always a question about one state's statute, so start from that state's page — [estate planning by state](/estate-planning).

## Frequently asked questions

**What does Uniform Probate Code 2-502 require?**
A writing, a signature, and at least two witnesses to the signing or the testator's acknowledgment ([MGL c.190B § 2-502(a)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleII/Chapter190B/Section2-502)).

**Do the witnesses have to be in the same room?**
Not under § 2-502 — no same-room, same-time, or in-the-testator's-presence clause. Florida, not a UPC state, does require it ([Fla. Stat. § 732.502(1)(c)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/732.502)).

**Is a handwritten will valid under 2-502?**
Under the holographic subsection, yes, if the signature and material portions are handwritten — but Michigan also requires a date ([MCL 700.2502(2)](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-700-2502.pdf)), and Massachusetts's § 2-502 has no holographic exception at all.

**Is 2-502 the harmless error rule?**
No — that is § 2-503, which demands clear and convincing evidence of testamentary intent ([Utah Code § 75-2-503](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S503_1800010118000101.xml)). Massachusetts left its § 2-503 "Reserved," Minnesota's starts at March 13, 2020, and Maine's § 2-503 is the self-proved-will section.

**How many states use the Uniform Probate Code?**
[18 jurisdictions](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_probate_code) have enacted it in whole or in part per Cornell's LII — but no two enactments are identical, so the governing citation is always the state's own statute.

## Where to start

**Work from the three requirements, then check the version your own state enacted.** A writing, a signature, and two witnesses to the signing or the testator's acknowledgment is the whole of § 2-502(a) as enacted ([Utah Code § 75-2-502(1)](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/C75-2-S502_1800010118000101.xml)); everything else above is a state's edit to it — a date requirement here, a deleted holographic exception there, a harmless-error section that may be "Reserved" or may not exist at all.
